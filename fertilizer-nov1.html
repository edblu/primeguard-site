Miami-Dade's Fertilizer Ban Lifts November 1: What to Apply, and What Still Stays Off Your Lawn

The PrimeGuard TeamFor Miami-Dade and Broward homeownersNovember 1, 20265 minute read

For almost half the year, feeding a Miami-Dade lawn has been off limits. The county fertilizer ordinance bans fertilizers containing nitrogen and phosphorus from May 15 through October 31, because rainy season storms wash those nutrients off yards, into storm drains and canals, and out to Biscayne Bay, where they feed the algal blooms and seagrass die offs the county has been fighting for years.

That window closes October 31. From November 1, you can fertilize again. But "the ban lifted" does not mean "anything goes." Some of the ordinance runs all year, and November is exactly when homeowners get it wrong, because the lawn looks hungry after five and a half months and the instinct is to pour it on. Here is what the rules actually allow, in plain language.

What changes on November 1

Nitrogen is back on the table, within limits. The county caps nitrogen at four pounds per 1,000 square feet per calendar year, no more than one pound per 1,000 square feet in a single application of solid fertilizer, and no more than half a pound per 1,000 square feet per application of liquid fertilizer. Granular products containing nitrogen must be at least 65 percent slow release, which is the number to look for on the bag's guaranteed analysis label. Slow release is not marketing here, it is the rule, and it is also better for the lawn: the grass gets fed over weeks instead of getting one green spike and then starving again.

What does NOT change: phosphorus stays off unless a soil test says otherwise

This is the part most people miss. Fertilizer containing phosphorus is prohibited in Miami-Dade unless a soil test shows your soil is actually deficient in it. That rule is not seasonal. A standard "complete" fertilizer with a middle number on the bag is not legal to spread just because November arrived. South Florida soils rarely need phosphorus, so the honest first step before any fall feeding is a simple soil test. It costs very little, it tells you what your lawn actually lacks, and it keeps you on the right side of the ordinance.

The rules that never take a day off

  • Keep a 20 foot fertilizer free zone. No fertilizer within 20 feet of the top of the bank of any pond, lake, canal or stream, a stormwater drain, a wetland, a seawall, or Biscayne Bay.
  • Never apply to saturated soil, or to turf that is dead or not actively growing.
  • Never apply when a severe weather advisory is in effect, or when heavy rain of more than 2 inches in 24 hours is forecast. November still brings storm fronts, and fertilizer laid down before a washout is money in the canal.
  • Keep grass clippings and yard debris off streets, sidewalks, and storm drains. Clippings blown into the street carry the same nutrients straight to the water.
  • Use a deflector shield on the spreader near pavement, water, and the fertilizer free zones, and sweep any granules that land on concrete back onto the lawn.

A simple November plan for a typical Miami lawn

  1. 1

    Test first

    One soil test tells you whether phosphorus is even allowed, and how much nitrogen your lawn can use.

  2. 2

    Pick a compliant product

    Granular, at least 65 percent slow release nitrogen, zero phosphorus unless your test says otherwise.

  3. 3

    Apply once, at the label rate

    Stay inside the per application cap. One measured application in November beats two heavy ones.

  4. 4

    Time it to a dry stretch

    At least a couple of days, water it in lightly, and keep the 20 foot zones clean.

  5. 5

    Flooded in October? Recover first

    If your yard flooded during the October king tides, read our saltwater recovery post first. A lawn still flushing salt out of its root zone needs recovery before feeding, not fertilizer on top of stress.

One county note: these rules are Miami-Dade's, and they apply countywide including inside municipalities, which can add their own restrictions on top. Broward and Palm Beach County homeowners should check their local rules before applying, because the restricted periods differ by county.
