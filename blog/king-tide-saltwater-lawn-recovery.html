King Tide Flooded Your Miami Yard? Saltwater Lawn Recovery, and How to Prep for the November 6 to 9 Tides

The PrimeGuard TeamFor Miami-Dade and Broward homeownersOctober 29, 20265 minute read

The water is gone. The damage is not.

Miami-Dade just came through the highest tides of the year. The fall king tide window ran October 24 to 30, and the South Florida Water Management District put the annual maximum predicted peak on October 27. If your street flooded this week, your yard took in saltwater, even if it never looked deep. And here is the part most homeowners miss: the water itself is not what kills the lawn. The salt it leaves behind in the soil is.

Salt pulls moisture out of grass roots and keeps pulling for days after the street dries. That is why a yard can look fine on Tuesday and start yellowing the following week. The good news is that South Florida lawns are tougher than they look, and the recovery steps are simple. The order matters more than the effort.

First: flush the salt down, in cycles

The fastest, cheapest fix is fresh water. University of Florida IFAS guidance for salt contaminated soil is straightforward: irrigate to move the salt down and out of the root zone. Two to three inches of fresh water is normally far too much for a lawn, and in this one case it is exactly the point.

Do it in cycles, not all at once. Water deeply, let it soak and drain, then repeat. Flooding the soil in one shot pushes the oxygen out and invites root rot, which trades one problem for a worse one. Rain does this job for free when it comes; run the irrigation when it does not.

While the ground is still soft, clear whatever the tide dropped off: silt, leaves, branches, and debris sitting on the grass. Turf smothered under flood debris cannot photosynthesize, and that material rots fast in Miami heat.

Second: wait before you prune or panic

Salt damaged shrubs and plants often shed their leaves and then push new growth on their own. UF/IFAS is blunt about this one: wait and see how the plant starts to recover before pruning anything. Cutting too early removes growth the plant was about to save. If dead branches are still obvious once new growth has started, that is the moment to cut them back, not before.

The same patience applies to brown patches in the lawn. Yellowing and slowed growth after a salt event are common and often temporary. Give the flush two weeks before you decide any section is truly dead.

Third: fix the soil where the salt settled

If patches stay yellow and stunted after flushing, the sodium is still sitting in the soil. That is where gypsum earns its keep. Gypsum helps displace sodium and improves soil structure so the salt can keep moving down and away from the roots. UF/IFAS guidance puts gypsum at 20 to 40 pounds per hundred square feet for salt affected soil, applied on the surface of an existing lawn and watered in deeply. A simple soil test first tells you whether you need it and how much, so you are not guessing.

Aerating compacted, flooded spots also helps. Floodwater compacts soil and suffocates roots; opening it back up restores the oxygen healthy roots and soil microbes need.

One timing note on fertilizer: do not feed a stressed lawn back to life this week. Miami-Dade's rainy season fertilizer restrictions lift on November 1, and even then, a recovering lawn wants its salt flushed and its roots settled first. Feeding comes after recovery, not instead of it.

Next window: November 6 to 9, then November 23 to 26

This is not over. The county schedule has two more fall king tide windows: November 6 to 9 and November 23 to 26. Miami Beach high tides in the first window peak early morning and early evening on November 6, so low yards will see water before most people are awake.

Before November 6, walk your property with one question: what would float, pool, or clog?

  • Clear the curb, gutter, and drain line in front of your home so street water has somewhere to go.
  • Haul away fallen branches, palm fronds, and leaf piles instead of stacking them at the curb.
  • Know your low spots. The corner that pooled in October is the corner that pools in November, and it is the first place to check after.
  • Move lightweight planters, buckets, and patio gear up or in if your yard sits low.

When it is worth calling a pro

Call for help if standing water is still in the yard 48 hours after the tide, if whole sections of lawn die off after the two week recovery window, or if the drainage path off your property is blocked by debris or a collapsed swale. Those are drainage and soil problems, not mowing problems, and they get worse with every tide window that passes.
