Snowbirds Are Coming Back: How to Reopen Your South Florida Home and Yard Right

The PrimeGuard TeamFor Miami-Dade and Broward homeownersOctober 29, 20266 minute read

Welcome back. Now about the yard.

If your South Florida home sat empty from June to November, it just went through the hardest five months of the year without you: peak rain, peak heat, hurricane season, and the October king tides. The house usually survives fine. The yard keeps the receipts.

Here is what five months of summer does to a property nobody is watching, and the order to fix it in so you are not doing the same work twice.

What you are probably coming home to

  • Overgrowth everywhere. Grass that was cut in June is a jungle by November in this climate, and hedges, palms, and vines do not pause for absent owners. Expect fallen palm fronds in the beds, self-cleaning palms that never got cleaned, and walkways narrowed by shrubs that grew over them.
  • Clogged drains and swales. Leaf litter, fronds, and silt wash into the low paths where water leaves your property. After a summer of storms and the October king tides, those paths are often blocked, which is why a yard can hold standing water days after a normal rain.
  • Algae and mold on hard surfaces. Driveways, walkways, patios, and the shaded side of the house go green and slick in the humidity. It is more than ugly; wet algae on a walkway is a genuine slip hazard for the first walk to the front door.
  • An irrigation system that needs a check before it runs. Heads get buried under overgrowth, lines shift, and a system that sat idle can have clogged emitters or a zone that no longer reaches. Running it blind for a month wastes water and still leaves dry corners dead.
  • Plants that struggled alone. Potted and bedded plants that missed watering cycles through dry spells, and anything low that sat in tidal water in October, need a look before you decide what is dead and what is just stressed.

The reopening checklist, in priority order

Work it in this order. Each step makes the next one cheaper.

  1. 1

    Safety and access first

    Before anything pretty, make sure you can get in and around safely. Clear the driveway and the walk to the front door, cut back anything blocking steps, doors, and the AC unit, and check the walkway for slick algae before anyone walks it with luggage. If a limb came down in a storm and is resting on a fence, roof edge, or power line path, that is a stop and call item, not a weekend project.

  2. 2

    Drainage second

    Walk the property line after or during a rain if you can and watch where the water goes. Clear the swale, the drain line, and the curb path in front of the home of fronds, leaves, and silt. A yard that cannot drain will undo every lawn fix you pay for after it.

  3. 3

    Lawn recovery third

    Once the yard is cleared and draining, the lawn gets its turn. First cut should take the overgrowth down in stages rather than scalping it to the dirt in one pass; grass that grew tall all summer has its growth point high, and cutting it to the ground in one shot shocks it. If your street took tidal water in October, flush the low spots with fresh water in cycles before anything else: salt left in the soil is what kills grass after a flood, and two to three inches of irrigation water, applied in soak and drain cycles, moves it down past the roots. Then give stressed sections two weeks before deciding they are dead. Yellowing after salt or neglect is often temporary.

  4. 4

    Curb appeal last

    Hedges shaped, palms cleaned up, beds re-edged, fresh mulch where it washed thin, and the entry walk cleaned. This is the step neighbors notice, and it only holds if the first three steps were done in order.

What to do yourself, and what to hand to a crew

Reasonable weekend work for most owners: picking up fronds and debris, clearing a blocked drain grate, checking each irrigation zone for a minute and noting which heads fail, rinsing salt off low grass after tidal flooding, and the patience jobs, like waiting to see which plants push new growth before pruning them.

Hand these to a crew: the first heavy cut and haul-away after months of overgrowth, palm cleanup up high, any limb work, shaping overgrown hedges back hard, and drainage problems where water still stands 48 hours after rain. Those need equipment, a truck for the debris, and, for anything overhead, people who do it every week.
